In May of 2026 I applied to a position at the Joe Gibbs Human Performance Institute. The institute is the AI group inside Joe Gibbs Racing's NASCAR operation. I was, simultaneously, applying to the APL track at Johns Hopkins for the fall of 2026. To anyone reading both applications side by side, these would look like different people. They are not.

This is the post about why both make sense.

## The thing I wrote on the application.

The application asked, plainly: *why do you want to work for a race team?* I wrote back that motorsports is one of the few fields where you win together and you lose together — that the team-first culture is the culture I've been chasing since I played football at Presbyterian, that I've spent years maintaining and modifying my own vehicles, and that I missed being part of a team that shares its outcomes on the same day.

All of that is true. None of it is the whole story.

## What's actually going on.

The longer truth is that **the kind of engineering I find interesting is the kind that has a scoreboard**. Banking infrastructure has scoreboards too — the patch cycle ran clean or it didn't, the system stayed up or it didn't — but the scoreboards are mostly private. The auditors see them. The risk officers see them. The customers don't, unless something breaks badly.

Motorsports has a scoreboard everyone watches. So does national-security cyber work, in a different sense — the systems either hold or they don't, and the consequences of "don't" are public in a way enterprise IT mostly isn't. **Both fields ask the same question of an engineer: did your system work today?** The answer is binary and the answer is observed.

The defense work matters more in absolute terms. I want to be clear about that. *Non Sibi Sed Patriae* is on my resume because I mean it. But the appeal of being inside a race team — a literal *team*, with a uniform and a paddock and a Sunday — is that it's the most concentrated, most observable form of the same engineering discipline.

## The 2021 Abu Dhabi Grand Prix.

The application also asked about my favorite race. I told them about the 2021 Abu Dhabi GP — Verstappen versus Hamilton, championship on the line, decided on a single lap on fresh softs after the safety car closed an eight-month gap to nothing.

What I didn't have room to write on the application was *why* that race specifically. It's not because of who won. It's because of what the race exposed about the work everyone had done in the eight months leading up to it. Two championship-grade engineering programs, two championship-grade drivers, and an entire season distilled into twelve corners where every decision — every pit stop, every strategy call, every aerodynamic compromise made in February — was finally graded. That's the version of engineering I want to be inside of.

## The cars in my garage.

The cars I've owned — a '94 Jeep YJ, a '15 Scion FRS, a '21 Toyota Supra — taught me three different things. The Jeep taught me to use a wrench. The FRS taught me that body work is finish-craft, not assembly. The Supra taught me that modern cars are computers that happen to have engines, and that the line between mechanic and embedded systems engineer is increasingly imaginary. That last one is the one that pulled me toward the JGHPI role — a NASCAR car in 2026 is mostly a sensor and signal-bus system that happens to also burn fuel. The AI group's job is to read those signals into actionable models. That's recognizably the same work I did at Wells Fargo, with a different substrate.

## Why this is in the dossier and not in a cover letter.

Because I want to be honest about what I'm doing. **I'm building a career where the engineering is rigorous, the work is observable, and the team is real.** Defense and motorsport are two ways to get there. APL is the priority. JGR was an application I wrote with both hands and meant every word of. If you're reading this as a hiring manager somewhere else and the apparent contradiction concerns you, please ask me about it — I've thought about it more than the resume suggests.
