The most important application I have open in the second half of 2026 is to the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory — specifically, the RISE program for graduate students enrolled at Hopkins. I'm in the program. I'm eligible. And of all the lanes I'm pursuing, APL is the one most aligned with the work I want to be doing in five years.

This is a short note on why.

## What APL actually is.

APL is a federally funded research and development center (FFRDC) chartered to Johns Hopkins University. It does classified cybersecurity research, missile defense, space systems work, autonomy, and the underlying mathematics and signal-processing that holds all of that together. It is not a defense contractor in the conventional sense — it is closer to a national laboratory. Its budget comes mostly from the Navy. Its work is mostly unclassified at the perimeter and increasingly classified the deeper you go.

The Cybersecurity M.S. at the Whiting School of Engineering exists in significant part because APL needs engineers who have been trained on its kinds of problems.

## Why I'm applying.

Three reasons, in order.

**First, the work is the closest analog in the US to "national-scale infrastructure cybersecurity research."** It is the work I would want to be doing if I had a clean slate to choose any cyber role in the country. The fact that it is geographically attached to the school I'm already enrolled in is good fortune, not strategy.

**Second, the clearance path is the cleanest I have access to.** APL sponsors clearances for the right candidates as a matter of normal hiring. My eligibility today plus enrollment at JHU plus the standard SF-86 process becomes an active Secret or TS clearance within roughly twelve months of starting. That clearance becomes the foundation for every other career move I make for the next decade.

**Third, the alumni network from RISE is the network I want.** Engineers who came up through APL are the people who staff MITRE, MIT Lincoln Lab, the major defense primes, the Tier 1 defense startups, and large fractions of the federal-cyber consulting bench. Being in that network early matters more than being early to any one company.

## What I'm doing this semester.

A few things, deliberately:

- **Coursework selection:** the strongest signal of "I am a serious cyber candidate" is what classes I'm taking in semester one. I'm prioritizing the courses that map to APL's mission areas — adversarial ML, network defense, cryptographic engineering — over the broader management-flavored options.
- **Independent skill-building:** I'm working through Hack The Box Pro Labs on weekends. APL recruiters notice this signal. It is not a substitute for the academic work; it is a complement.
- **The home lab as a research environment:** the Proxmox + R610 setup I've been running for years is becoming the place I prototype anything I want to discuss in an interview. Self-hosted versions of the systems I want to study at APL.
- **The portfolio you're reading:** every transmission, every project writeup, every architecture diagram on this site is something I can point a recruiter to. APL specifically values engineers who can communicate. Writing here is part of being that candidate.

## What I'd say to anyone else in my position.

If you are also a JHU grad student reading this — apply to RISE. The application is rolling. The early-consideration deadline is March 31. There is no good reason to wait, and there are several good reasons not to.

If you are a non-JHU candidate reading this and considering whether to pursue an M.S. for APL access specifically: it is one of the few cases where the credential is a real and direct pipeline. The school has its own value. The proximity to APL is the multiplier.

If you are someone at APL reading this — I'm in your inbox. I'd welcome the conversation.
