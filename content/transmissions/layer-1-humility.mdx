In February of 2025 I was an Assistant Vice President at Wells Fargo, running patch management and CVE remediation across more than ten thousand servers. In May of 2025 I was in the dirt outside of Charlotte, pulling fiber for a residential ISP.

This is the post about what happened between those two sentences, and what I learned from it.

## The setup.

I was laid off in February. The layoff was not personal — it was a reorganization, and reorganizations take people who happen to be sitting in the wrong org chart at the wrong time. I had savings, a respectable resume, and a few months of runway. I could have moved laterally into another bank, taken a senior infrastructure role at a fintech, or gone all-in on the cyber masters I'd already applied to at Johns Hopkins.

I did none of those things. I picked up a job splicing fiber at Prince Telecom and later doing outside plant work at ITG Communications.

If you've never spliced fiber: it is messy, repetitive, physically demanding work that involves a lot of crawling under porches, working around fire-ant mounds, and learning to identify the specific dialect of frustration that arrives when a fusion splicer's screen says `LOSS: 0.41 dB` and you needed `0.10 dB`. It is also, in a way I did not expect, **the work that closes the loop on most of what I'd been doing at a desk for the prior three years.**

## Why I did this.

The honest answer is that I needed to remember what the physical layer feels like.

It is very easy, when you spend three years inside a bank's infrastructure, to slowly forget that every cloud has a floor. The "cloud" is a building. The building has a generator. The generator has a fuel pump. The fuel pump runs on AC pulled from a substation. The substation is fed by transmission lines. The transmission lines are protected by SCADA systems that someone has to keep patched. **Every abstraction has an underside, and most of us never see it.**

I had spent three years writing Ansible playbooks that pushed CVEs across ten thousand servers and never once asked where the servers were physically plugged in. So I went and learned.

## What I actually learned.

A few things that have stuck.

### 1. The fiber doesn't care about your title.

The first day on the job, I was working with a crew of guys who had been doing this for twenty years. They did not care that I had been an AVP at a bank. They cared whether I could prep a splice tray correctly the first time, and whether I would whine about the heat.

This sounds like a humility lesson, and it is. But it's also a *competence* lesson. There is a way of being good at a job that has nothing to do with prestige and everything to do with hands. I had been good at my Wells Fargo job, but in a way that was deeply legible only to people inside the same prestige system. **The fiber crew had a kind of competence that translates across any system, any title, any geography.** That was worth a year of my time.

### 2. The OSI model has a layer one for a reason.

I learned the OSI model in college. I taught it to junior engineers at Wells Fargo. I had never, before 2025, *put my hands on layer one* in any sustained way. Now I have. And I can tell you that a lot of "network problems" that escalate up to senior engineers are layer-one problems that nobody bothered to check. A bad jumper. A dirty connector. A cable that someone stepped on six years ago and that the building's been routing around ever since.

Future me will check the physical layer first. It costs me nothing to look. It costs hours when I don't.

### 3. Layer 1 humility is not the same as Layer 1 *apologia.*

I was tempted, for a few months, to frame this year as "I took a step down to learn humility." That framing is wrong, and it does the fiber crew a disservice. Fiber splicing is not a step *down* — it's a step *over*, into a different and harder kind of competence than enterprise IT. **The work is harder, not easier. The pay is lower because the labor market values prestige work, not because the work is less valuable.**

I am going back to enterprise infrastructure now, and I am going back as a better engineer for the year, but I am not going back because the fiber work was beneath me. It was not.

### 4. The bank version of me would have made a worse cyber researcher.

I'm about to start a Master's in Cybersecurity at Johns Hopkins. Specifically, the APL track. The work APL does — on physical security, on critical infrastructure cyber, on the actual *plumbing* of national-security systems — would have been less accessible to me, intellectually, before the fiber year than after it.

There is a kind of engineer who only knows the cloud, and there is a kind of engineer who knows the cloud *and* what runs underneath it. The second kind is rarer, more useful, and harder to fake. The fiber year was an investment in being the second kind.

## What I'd say to anyone considering doing this.

A few things.

**One:** if you have the savings and the runway, doing a year of work that is physically harder than your desk job will reset your relationship with desk work in a way nothing else will. You will come back wanting to be in front of a screen and grateful for it, which is a healthier baseline than the slow-burn resentment most knowledge workers carry around.

**Two:** do not romanticize the trades. The crews I worked with are good at their jobs and proud of their work, and they would rather be paid the white-collar wage than be paid the working-class wage. The cosplay version of "left tech to work with my hands" is not what this was. This was *adding* a layer of skill, not subtracting one.

**Three:** the year does not appear on most resumes the way it appeared in mine. I put it on. I led with it. **It was a feature, not a footnote.** The companies that wanted to talk to me after the year were the ones who understood why it mattered. The companies that didn't, didn't.
