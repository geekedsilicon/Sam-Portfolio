## How this started.

I was twelve, trying to host a Minecraft server so my friends could join. The router didn't cooperate. I found *Hamachi* — an old peer-to-peer tunneling tool — and got the server up. Then I figured out port forwarding so we wouldn't need Hamachi. Then I figured out why port forwarding was dangerous. Then I started reading about NAT, about what a public IP actually was, about how the internet routed packets across the world to get to a kid's PC in Woodruff, South Carolina.

That sequence — *I want to make a thing work → I learn how it actually works → I learn why what I just did is risky* — is the entire shape of my career.

By the time I was in high school I was building custom PCs for friends and modding game communities. By the time I was at USC I was researching P4 packet-forwarding under the Office of Naval Research. By the time I was at Wells Fargo I was managing CVE remediation across 10,000+ servers. The substrate changed. The instinct didn't.

Today, I do the same thing, on bigger systems, with higher stakes.

<AsciiBox title="WHAT THE 12-YEAR-OLD VERSION WAS ACTUALLY LEARNING">

- VPN tunneling protocols (started with Hamachi; later WireGuard, OpenVPN)
- NAT, port forwarding, and the actual mechanics of how home networks connect to the internet
- The risk surface of opening ports — and the dangers of trusting modded clients and custom communities
- Hardware and its limitations: what your CPU, RAM, and uplink could actually sustain
- Why hosting your own service teaches you more than using someone else's
- Eventually: bug bounty programs, Hack The Box, Kali Linux, and the discipline of breaking things on purpose

</AsciiBox>

## What I do now.

I'm a cloud security and infrastructure engineer. At Wells Fargo I ran patch management and CVE remediation across more than ten thousand Windows and Linux servers, automated the pipeline with Ansible and Jenkins, and built AI agents that took thousands of hours of repetitive engineering work off the team's plate. None of that work would have been possible without the home lab. None of the home lab would have been possible without the Minecraft server.

I'm now pursuing a Master's in Cybersecurity at Johns Hopkins, on the APL track. I'm a US citizen, eligible for security clearance. I work in a country I take seriously, on systems I take seriously, with the people who take both seriously.

When I'm not at the keyboard I'm in the garage. The cars I've built — a 1994 Jeep YJ, a 2015 Scion FRS, a 2021 Toyota Supra — are the same skills as the servers in different mediums. Fabrication, electrical, signal busses, refusing to skip the alignment step.

## The three things I try to be.

<LatinMotto translate>Audacia et Veritas</LatinMotto>

### Audacity and truth.

I make bold claims and I back them with receipts. Every number on this site is defensible. Every system I've built has a diagram, a repo, or a working demo. I would rather say *one* hard true thing about my work than ten soft impressive-sounding things.

The reason this matters: most engineering candidates either oversell — and get caught in interviews — or undersell — and never get to the interview at all. I aim for the third option: claim exactly what I did, and prove it.

<LatinMotto translate>Per Aspera Ad Astra</LatinMotto>

### Through hardship to the stars.

The career arc, briefly: I built custom PCs at thirteen for ninety-nine dollars a build. I worked as a lifeguard, a cashier, a mover, an overnight stocker through high school and college. I was a research assistant at USC under Office of Naval Research funding, doing packet-forwarding work in a programming language with roughly five hundred experts in the world. I made it to Assistant Vice President at Wells Fargo by my mid-twenties. I was laid off in early 2025 and went and spliced fiber in the dirt for a year because the work needed doing and I needed to remember what the physical layer feels like.

There is no skipping any of these steps. The fiber year is not in spite of the bank year. The lifeguard summers are not in spite of the engineering degree. **You become the engineer who can hold a CISO's attention by also being the engineer who knows what a strain relief looks like when it's done wrong.**

The path is the path. *Per aspera, ad astra.*

<LatinMotto translate>Non Sibi Sed Patriae</LatinMotto>

### Not for self, but for country.

The most personal of the three. I want to do work that matters at a national scale.

Cybersecurity is one of the few fields where private-sector engineering and national-security work are genuinely the same craft — the same buffer overflow, the same misconfigured S3 bucket, the same lateral-movement pattern, whether it's threatening a bank or a power grid or a satellite constellation. I want to be on the side of the engineers defending those systems.

I'm in the JHU program because APL is where that line is most directly walked. I'm eligible for clearance because the work I want to do requires it. I post my callsign in Latin because my heroes mostly did.

## The team part of all of this.

I played football at Presbyterian College on a scholarship before I moved to USC. People who know me from engineering sometimes don't know that part of my history, and people who know me from football sometimes don't know about the engineering. They are the same person. The same instinct that wanted to be in a locker room on Saturday wants to be in a paddock on Sunday and wants to be in an APL lab on Monday: I work better when there is a team that shares the outcome with me. *Non sibi sed patriae* extends to the team in the room as much as to the country outside it.

In May of 2026 I wrote an application to the AI group at Joe Gibbs Racing — a NASCAR shop fifteen minutes from my house. I also wrote an application to APL. Both were sincere. Both are explained, in different ways, by the same impulse. If the apparent contradiction interests you, [there's a longer note](/transmissions/why-motorsport) about it.
